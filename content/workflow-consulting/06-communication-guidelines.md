# Communication Guidelines

Two layers here. GPC's baseline rules for which channel carries what, and how
Grant himself actually writes to clients in email, pulled from real sent
messages the same way the Grant Way playbook pulls patterns from real call
transcripts. The second layer is a starting sample, not a finished study. Add
to it as more threads come in.

## Which channel, for what

GPC runs four communication tools, each with a job. Mixing them up is how
requirements, deadlines, and decisions go missing.

- **Slack** is the internal hub: questions, updates, team announcements,
  tools and wins, and client communication only when that client has an
  active shared Slack channel.
- **ClickUp** is where client work lives. Every engaged client gets a
  ClickUp chat channel at the folder level. Use it for tasks, project
  updates, requirements, blockers, deadlines, and deliverable status.
- **HubSpot** is where sales activity lives: calls, meetings, sales emails,
  deal notes, and pipeline updates. Run sales activity through HubSpot or a
  connected tool so it logs automatically.
- **Email** fills the gap: a client who doesn't use Slack, a formal or
  long-form message, or an external stakeholder outside the normal
  collaboration tools.

The simple rule: Slack is where we communicate, ClickUp is where we manage
client work, HubSpot is where we manage sales activity, and email fills the
external gaps. If a conversation in any of these changes the work, creates a
requirement, or affects a deal, it gets captured in the tool that owns that
kind of record, not left to live only in the channel it happened in.

## The 1-3-1 method for problem resolution

When something breaks or a requirement wasn't fully known at build time, this
is the shape of the response: one problem, three solutions, one
recommendation.

1. **One problem.** State it in one or two sentences: name the thing that's
   affected and what went wrong. Don't propose a solution yet.
2. **Three solutions.** Stretch past the first answer that comes to mind.
3. **One recommendation.** Weigh the three, name the one you'd move forward
   with, and say why.

Clients notice the thoughtfulness this creates, especially right after an
error. If more than one problem surfaced, run the method once per problem
rather than blending them into one response.

For an automation error specifically: lead every response, proactive or
reactive, with one plain sentence naming what the automation does before
anything else. Acknowledge the client's frustration directly on a
financial-impact error. Never blame the client's data before investigating.
If a fix will take more than ten minutes, create a ClickUp task and tag the
Operations Manager rather than going quiet while you work it.

## How Grant actually writes to clients

Everything below is observed from real sent email to Rush Enterprises,
Coffee & Clothes, and Cooper Erving & Savage, not a style guide someone
wrote from theory. Study the pattern, not the exact words.

**First name, no formal salutation.** "Hey Jason," "Hey RJ," "Hey Amber,"
even to a senior enterprise contact he's never met in person. Sign-off is
"Grant," never "Regards" or "Sincerely."

**The first sentence answers the reason for the email.** No warm-up, no "I
hope this finds you well." A scheduling reply opens with which time works.
A contract update opens with what changed. A data request opens with what's
being asked for.

**Short paragraphs, one idea each, separated by a blank line.** Even the
longer emails in the sample run three or four short paragraphs, never a
wall of text.

**Scheduling options are a bulleted list with the time zone stated every
time**, not folded into a sentence:

```
Do any of these times (CDT) work?

- tomorrow (Sep 15): 12:30-1:30 pm, 4:00-5:00 pm
- Wed Sep 16: 1:00-2:00 pm
- Thu Sep 17: 12:30-1:30 pm
```

**Real internal context gets shared plainly, not deflected.** Told a client
sponsor directly that a launch date moved because of a shift in AI market
sentiment that week, and named whose read on it he trusted. That candor is
the point, not a slip: it's what makes the client trust the next update too.

**One line acknowledges the other person's point before moving on.** "Heard
on lead time, we're in lock step!" comes before the substance of the reply,
not after.

**A confirmation can be one line.** When nothing more needs saying, "Confirmed
on our end. Thank you!" is the whole email.

**Offers direct access when responsiveness matters.** A cell number and an
availability window ("I'll be around until about 9PM EST") accompanies a
time-sensitive request, so the client isn't stuck waiting on email.

**The sign-off line rotates and stays warm, never generic.** "Thank you
kindly and hope you had a great weekend," "Have a great week ahead," "Be
well," "Thank you for coordinating." It's a real one-line close before the
signature block, not a boilerplate "Best regards."

**The signature block itself never varies:**

```
Best,
Grant Hushek
CEO, Grantbot & GPC

Sent via Superhuman
```

One honest exception worth naming: GPC's own authored documents ban the em
dash outright (see `gpc-skills/skills/gpc-brand-docs/references/voice-and-slop.md`).
Grant's actual informal client email doesn't always follow that: one sampled
email opens "Hey Amber — Friday 9/18 at 11:00am ET works." This module
describes what he does, not a rule to import elsewhere. The em dash ban
stays in force for anything GPC drafts as a formal deliverable.
